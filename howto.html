How to play Gonu

Gonu (고누) is a whole family of Korean folk board games — more than forty known variants, traditionally scratched into dirt yards rather than printed in a rulebook. PlayGonu's main Gonu game is Cham-gonu, a genuinely deep one; Jul-gonu and the other variants below are shorter warm-up rounds. Omok (Korean Gomoku) is a separate classic (not part of the Gonu family) added alongside them — here's how each works.

Cham-gonu 참고누 The main game
  • Each player has 12 pieces. Take turns placing one at a time on any empty point of a 24-point board (three nested squares, corners and side-midpoints connected) — this is the first half of the game.
  • Line up 3 of your own pieces in a row — a "mill" — to capture an enemy piece. A point used in a capture is marked and locked for the rest of placement.
  • Once all 24 pieces are placed, the second half begins: slide one piece per turn to an adjacent empty point, still hunting for mills.
  • Win by reducing your opponent to 2 pieces, or leaving them with no legal move. This is the same family of game as the Western "Nine Men's Morris."
  • If all 24 points fill up while placing and nobody has made a mill, the game is a draw.
  • In the moving phase, if 20 moves in a row (both sides counted) pass with no capture, the side with more pieces left wins (equal pieces is a draw) — a PlayGonu rule.
  • Western rules (optional): pick “Western rules” on the Cham-gonu page to play by Twelve Men's Morris rules instead. A captured point is not locked during placement, a player down to exactly 3 pieces may fly to any empty point, and the 20-move rule is replaced by a draw if someone is stuck on 3 pieces and nobody captures for 10 moves each.

Jul-gonu 줄고누 Warm-up round
  • Each player starts with 4 pieces lined up on their own edge of a 4×4 line board.
  • Move one piece per turn, one step along a line.
  • Trap an enemy piece between two of yours in a straight line to capture it.
  • Win by reducing your opponent to one piece, or leaving them with no legal move.
  • If 30 moves in a row (both sides counted) pass with no capture, the side with more pieces left wins (equal pieces is a draw).

Daseotjul-gonu 다섯줄고누 Jul-gonu family
  • Same rules as Jul-gonu, one size bigger: 5 pieces each on a 5×5-point board.
  • Move one piece per turn, one step along a line; sandwich an enemy piece between two of yours to capture it.
  • Win by reducing your opponent to one piece, or leaving them with no legal move.
  • If 30 moves in a row (both sides counted) pass with no capture, the side with more pieces left wins (equal pieces is a draw).

Palpal-gonu 팔팔고누 Jul-gonu family
  • The widest Jul-gonu-family board — 8 pieces each on an 8×8-point grid.
  • Same sandwich-capture rules as Jul-gonu and Daseotjul-gonu, just with a lot more room to maneuver.
  • Win by reducing your opponent to one piece, or leaving them with no legal move.
  • If 60 moves in a row (both sides counted) pass with no capture, the side with more pieces left wins (equal pieces is a draw).

Bakwi-gonu 바퀴고누 More strategy
  • 4 pieces each, clustered at opposite corners of a 4×4-point board.
  • A normal move is one step — but a piece standing on one of the board's four corner "wheels" may instead spin and slide any distance in a straight line.
  • A wheel slide that runs into an enemy piece captures it — the only way to capture in this game.
  • Win by reducing your opponent to one piece, or leaving them with no legal move.
  • If 30 moves in a row (both sides counted) pass with no capture, the side with more pieces left wins (equal pieces is a draw).

Omok 오목 (Korean Gomoku) Not a Gonu variant
  • Players alternate placing one stone per turn on any empty intersection of a 15×15 board.
  • Get 5 of your own stones in a row — any direction — to win. No captures.
  • Choose Freestyle (any 5-or-more wins) or Standard (Renju), where the first player can't play certain "forbidden points" (double-three, double-four, six-or-more in a row) — the board marks them with an ×. In Renju, Black's first stone goes on the center point, and broken threes and fours (with a one-point gap) count toward the forbidden shapes too.
  • 225 moves with no winner is a draw.

Strategy tips
  • Control the center. Central points connect to more lines, so a piece there has more places to move — and more lines along which to set up a capture.
  • Watch where you walk. Moving a piece between two enemy pieces doesn't capture it — only the piece that just moved can trigger a capture. But check the reverse: don't hand your opponent a free sandwich on their turn.
  • Don't trade blind. In the capture variants, swapping pieces evenly is rarely free — it usually opens a line for whoever moves next.
  • In Cham-gonu, don't rush to form a mill during placement. A mill you can open and re-close later (by moving a piece out and straight back in) captures again and again — that's worth more than one early capture.
Playing vs AI

Every variant has a built-in computer opponent (no server, no download) with four difficulty levels. Easy mostly looks one move ahead; Master thinks several moves deep and plays a genuinely tough game. You can also choose to play first or second.
