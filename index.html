How Cham-gonu works
  • Each player has 12 pieces. Take turns placing one at a time on any empty point of the 24-point board — placement is the first half of the game.
  • Line up 3 of your pieces in a row (straight, along the drawn lines) to form a "mill" — then remove one enemy piece. A point used in a capture is marked and locked for the rest of placement.
  • Once all 24 pieces are placed, the second half begins: slide one piece per turn to an adjacent empty point, still forming mills to capture.
  • Win by reducing your opponent to 2 pieces, or leaving them with no legal move.
  • If all 24 points fill up while placing and nobody has made a mill, the game is a draw.
  • In the moving phase, if 20 moves in a row (both sides counted) pass with no capture, the side with more pieces left wins (equal pieces is a draw) — a PlayGonu rule.
  • Western rules (optional): pick “Western rules” above the board to play by Twelve Men's Morris rules instead. A captured point is not locked during placement, a player down to exactly 3 pieces may fly to any empty point, and the 20-move rule is replaced by a draw if someone is stuck on 3 pieces and nobody captures for 10 moves each.
  • Deciding who goes first (쟁두): traditionally, two players play rock-paper-scissors, and the loser gets to move first.

Gonu Games Online: Cham-gonu, the Korean Twelve Men's Morris

Gonu (고누) is Korea's family of traditional board games, and Cham-gonu (참고누) is its Nine Men's Morris–style member: a two-player strategy game that is the Korean relative of Nine Men's Morris. Each player places 12 pieces on a board of three nested squares, lines up three in a row — a "mill" — to remove an enemy piece, and then slides pieces point to point in a second phase. There are no dice and no hidden information, so the better plan wins. On PlayGonu you can play it in your browser against an AI or a friend, with no download and no account.

Gonu and Cham-gonu: where it comes from

Gonu is a family of Korean folk board games. Players draw a board on the ground or on paper and use pebbles or twigs as pieces, so no special equipment is needed. That is why the games spread across the whole country and carried regional names such as goni and kkoni (Gyeonggi), kkon and kkonu (Jeolla), kkon (Gyeongsang) and kkonja (Jeju). The name is thought to come from an old word meaning "to contend". A board carved into an artifact from a Goryeo-era celadon kiln suggests people were playing at least as far back as the Goryeo period (918–1392), and Kim Hong-do's late-Joseon genre painting "Gonu Play" shows a group playing on a board drawn in the dirt.

Cham-gonu is the Gonu game that looks most like a Western one. Nine Men's Morris is one of the most widely traveled board games in history, with boards found from ancient temples to medieval cathedral cloisters. Its twelve-piece form, Twelve Men's Morris, adds four diagonal lines to the board and gives each player twelve pieces, which is exactly the shape of Cham-gonu's board. In South Africa the same family is played as Morabaraba. English-language Wikipedia lists Cham-gonu among the Gonu games under the name Gonjilgonu (참고누), and the Korean encyclopedias record it as 곤질고누 because players call "kkon" when they complete a mill. According to the Ministry of Culture, Sports and Tourism, a board was carved on an artifact from a celadon kiln site in Jinan County, which makes it one of the older Gonu. Cham-gonu is a Korean game in its own right, not a copy: the details of the rules below are the ones PlayGonu uses. Our comparison with Nine Men's Morris goes through the differences in more depth.

How to play Cham-gonu

  1. Who goes first: Traditionally the players play rock-paper-scissors and the loser moves first.
  2. Placement (first half): Take turns placing one piece on any empty point.
  3. Mills: When three of your pieces stand in a straight line along the drawn lines, you remove one enemy piece. A point where a piece was captured is marked, and nobody can place there for the rest of placement.
  4. Movement (second half): Once all 24 pieces are down, take turns sliding one piece along a line to an adjacent empty point. Forming a mill still captures.
  5. Winning: Reduce your opponent to 2 pieces, or leave them with no legal move.
  6. Draws and the move limit: If the board fills up during placement and nobody has formed a mill, the game is a draw. In the movement phase, if 20 moves in a row (both sides counted) pass with no capture, the side with more pieces wins; equal pieces is a draw (a PlayGonu rule).
  7. Western rules (optional): The game screen has a rules selector. Choosing “Western rules” switches to Twelve Men's Morris rules: captured points are not locked during placement, a player with exactly 3 pieces may fly to any empty point, and instead of the 20-move rule the game is drawn if someone is stuck on 3 pieces and nobody captures for 10 moves each.

Cham-gonu tips

  • Fight for the middle square. Its points have lines in four directions, so they offer the most mills and the most room to move.
  • Block two-in-a-row early. When two enemy pieces stand on a line, the third point is the one to take away from them.
  • Place with the second half in mind. If your pieces end up boxed in when placement ends, you may have no legal move and lose. Keep pieces next to open points.
  • Build a running mill. A piece that swings back and forth between two lines, forming a mill on every move, decides most games. See our running-mill guide.

Frequently asked questions

Is Cham-gonu the same as Nine Men's Morris?

They belong to the same family: form three in a row to capture, in a placing phase and then a moving phase. Cham-gonu uses 12 pieces and a board with diagonal lines, which matches Twelve Men's Morris more closely than the standard nine-piece game.

What is a "mill"?

Three of your pieces in a straight line along the drawn lines. Forming one lets you remove an enemy piece. Korean players call out "kkon" when they make one.

Can Cham-gonu end in a draw?

Yes. If the board fills up during placement without a single mill, it is a draw, and so is a movement phase that reaches the 20-move limit with equal pieces.
