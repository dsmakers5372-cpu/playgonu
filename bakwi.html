How Bakwi-gonu works
  • Each player starts with 4 pieces clustered at their own corner of a 4x4-point board.
  • On a normal turn, move one piece a single step along a line.
  • The four corners are "wheels" — a piece standing on one may instead spin and slide any distance in a straight line.
  • A wheel slide that runs into an enemy piece captures it and stops there. That's the only way to capture in this game — a plain step never does.
  • Win by reducing your opponent to one piece, or leaving them with no legal move.
  • If 30 moves in a row (both sides counted) pass with no capture, the side with more pieces left wins (equal pieces is a draw).
The best-documented new addition

Unlike several other variants added alongside it, Bakwi-gonu's rules are confirmed by two independent sources — the wheel-slide capture is the real, documented mechanic, not a guess.

Bakwi-gonu — a Korean Traditional Board Game, Play It Online

Bakwi-gonu (바퀴고누, "wheel Gonu") is a traditional Korean board game in which you spin around one of the four corner "wheels" of the board and slide in a straight line, capturing the first enemy piece you meet on that line. Normally pieces move one step at a time, but a piece standing on a wheel can run all the way across the board, so the fight is over who holds the wheels. Gonu and Bakwi-gonu: where it comes from

Gonu is a family of Korean folk board games. Players draw a board on the ground or on paper and use pebbles or twigs as pieces, so no special equipment is needed. That is why the games spread across the whole country and carried regional names such as goni and kkoni (Gyeonggi), kkon and kkonu (Jeolla), kkon (Gyeongsang) and kkonja (Jeju). The name is thought to come from an old word meaning "to contend". A board carved into an artifact from a Goryeo-era celadon kiln suggests people were playing at least as far back as the Goryeo period (918–1392), and Kim Hong-do's late-Joseon genre painting "Gonu Play" shows a group playing on a board drawn in the dirt.

Gonu games that capture by going around a corner are named differently from region to region. The Digital Gwangju Culture Encyclopedia describes one as Jajeoncha-gonu ("bicycle Gonu"): each player puts four pieces on the board, and "to capture, a piece must go around one of the four corners and takes only the first piece it meets on that line". The round loops at the corners look like wheels, which is why it is also called Bakwi-gonu. In that record the winner must capture every enemy piece, whereas PlayGonu follows its other Gonu games and lets you win by reducing your opponent to one piece.

How to play Bakwi-gonu

  1. Setup: Each player gathers 4 pieces toward their own corners, facing the opponent.
  2. Moving: Normally, slide one piece one step along a line, horizontally or vertically. A one-step move cannot capture.
  3. Wheels: A piece on a corner (a wheel) can instead spin around the wheel and slide any distance along a straight line. It stops in front of any piece in its path.
  4. Capturing: If the first piece the slide meets is an enemy piece, you capture it and stop on its square. This is the only way to capture.
  5. Winning: Leave your opponent with just 1 piece, or with no piece that can move.
  6. Move limit: If 30 moves in total pass with no capture, the side with more pieces wins; equal pieces is a draw (a PlayGonu rule).

Bakwi-gonu tips

  • Take a wheel first. Only a piece on a wheel can capture, so the side holding the wheels has the initiative.
  • Stay off the enemy wheel's line. If your piece is first on an edge line running from a wheel the enemy holds, it will be captured at once.
  • Block with your own pieces. A slide stops at the first piece it meets, so a piece placed in front protects the ones behind it.
  • Think about where you land. A capturing piece stops on the captured square. If that square is on an enemy wheel line, you can be captured right back.

Frequently asked questions

What is the "wheel" in Bakwi-gonu?

It is the round loop drawn at each of the board's four corners. A piece standing on one can spin around it and slide a long way in a straight line.

Can I capture with a one-step move?

No. Captures happen only when a piece spins around a wheel and slides.

Is it the same game as Jajeoncha-gonu?

A Gwangju record describes the same method under the name Jajeoncha-gonu: go around a corner and take the first piece on that line. Details such as the winning condition differ by region.

Can a game end in a draw?

Yes, if 30 moves pass with no capture and both sides have the same number of pieces.
